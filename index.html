A · Madness in Wonderland

Lewis Carroll · Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865) · Skywalks 1ère, p. 96.

Read the text in a larger, selectable format

“Would you tell me,” said Alice, a little timidly, “why you are painting those roses?”

Five and Seven said nothing, but looked at Two. Two began in a low voice, “Why the fact is, you see, Miss, this here ought to have been a red rose-tree, and we put a white one in by mistake; and if the Queen was to find it out, we should all have our heads cut off, you know. So you see, Miss, we’re doing our best, afore she comes, to—” At this moment Five, who had been anxiously looking across the garden, called out “The Queen! The Queen!” and the three gardeners instantly threw themselves flat upon their faces. [...]

First came ten soldiers carrying clubs, these were all shaped like the three gardeners, oblong and flat, with their hands and feet at the corners: next the ten courtiers; these were ornamented all over with diamonds, and walked two and two, as the soldiers did. After these came the royal children; there were ten of them [...]: they were all ornamented with hearts. Next came the guests, mostly Kings and Queens [...]; and, last of all this grand procession, came THE KING AND QUEEN OF HEARTS.

Alice was rather doubtful whether she ought not to lie down on her face like the three gardeners, but she could not remember ever having heard of such a rule at processions [...].

The Queen said severely “Who is this?” She said it to the Knave of Hearts, who only bowed and smiled in reply.

“Idiot!” said the Queen, tossing her head impatiently; and, turning to Alice, she went on, “What’s your name, child?”

“My name is Alice, so please your Majesty,” said Alice very politely; but she added, to herself, “Why, they’re only a pack of cards, after all. I needn’t be afraid of them!”

“And who are these?” said the Queen, pointing to the three gardeners who were lying round the rose-tree [...].

“How should I know?” said Alice, surprised at her own courage. “It’s no business of mine.”

The Queen [...] screamed “Off with her head! Off—”

“Nonsense!” said Alice, very loudly and decidedly, and the Queen was silent.

B · Brilliant Minds?

Lewis Helfand and Vinod Kumar · Gulliver’s Travels: The Graphic Novel (2010), adapted from Jonathan Swift’s novel (1726) · Skywalks 1ère, p. 97.

Read the panels in a larger, selectable format

School of languages

I also went to a school of languages and saw a scheme for completely abolishing all words as a great advantage to people’s health.

The reasoning

The logic was—every word we speak, to some degree, results in the dwindling of our lungs by corrosion, and contributes to the shortening of our lives.

The proposed solution

A solution was suggested that it would be more convenient for all men to carry with them all things necessary to express whatever they were supposed to speak about!

School of political projectors

I was most surprised when I went to the school of political projectors. There, a professor offered a wonderful device to reconcile violent parties.

The proposal

Take a hundred leaders of each party, and divide them into couples—one from each party. Then let two operators saw off the necks of each couple. Then equally divide and interchange the brains, applying each to the head of his opposite-party man.

The promised result

Two half brains being left to debate the matter within the space of one skull would soon come to a good understanding.

Gulliver’s reaction

I saw nothing in this country that could persuade me to stay longer, and I began to think of returning home to England.

Departure

I decided to head to the island of Luggnagg, southeast of Japan, in order to return to Europe.

Reading transcription of the supplied graphic novel page. Panel headings are reading aids. Dwindling = reduction · skull = cranium · rival parties = opposing political groups.

Help with the projects

to abolish words = supprimer les mots · a scheme = un projet · to reconcile = réconcilier · rival parties = partis politiques opposés.

Exchange checklist: identify the people, explain the strange rule or project, describe its logic and its limits, then explain the criticism. Your partners should be able to understand the document from your explanation.
In class · 8 min comparison + 5 min newsroom

Breaking News from Wonderland.

Keep the comparison

Carroll and Swift use absurd worlds to question authority. The Queen’s arbitrary orders expose the abuse of power. The scholars’ projects expose confidence in theories that ignore practical consequences. Neither scene is simply random: each follows a twisted line of reasoning.

A first headline, not the full final task

Choose one event from either document. Announce the official claim, then reveal its consequences. You may borrow an event. You do not have to invent a new world.

White Roses Banned!

Who gives the order? What must the gardeners do? Why are they afraid?

Words Abolished for Better Health!

What do the scholars claim? How will people communicate? What could go wrong?

Our final mission

In groups of three, prepare a 2–3 minute news report from Wonderland: a news anchor, a reporter and a witness. Present an absurd decision and reveal its consequences. The anchor and reporter can remain serious while the witness reacts. No video editing is required.

Useful language: The Queen has announced… / What worries the gardeners is… / This decision…, which… / According to our witness…

At home, complete the three language sections. Learn the vocabulary, check your answers, and keep a note of mistakes to revisit.

At home · Word Lab · about 15–20 minutes

Words that reveal the problem.

Tap a card to reveal its meaning. Change the mode to practise French meanings or English definitions. The cards also revisit the vocabulary of festivities and disorder from the previous lessons.

Use the right word

Word bank: abolish · arbitrary · impractical · reconcile · satire · wordplay

Useful distinctions

Humour is a broad category. Irony involves a gap between words and meaning or reality. Satire uses humour or exaggeration to criticise. Wordplay exploits meanings or sounds. Deadpan describes a serious delivery of funny material.

At home · Grammar 1 · about 15–20 minutes

What do you mean… which?

Le repère essentiel

WHAT signifie ici « ce que / ce qui ». Il inclut son antécédent : on peut souvent le remplacer par the thing(s) that.

What Alice says surprises the Queen.
Ce qu’Alice dit surprend la Reine.

La proposition What Alice says est le sujet de surprises. Dans Alice questions what the Queen says, la relative est complément de questions.

WHICH reprend un antécédent déjà exprimé. Il peut reprendre un nom non humain, ou une proposition entière.

The scheme which Gulliver describes is absurd.
Which reprend le nom the scheme.

The scholars abolish words, which makes communication harder.
Which reprend le fait de supprimer les mots.

Quand which commente la proposition précédente, on le sépare par une virgule. Ne pas confondre ces emplois relatifs avec what / which dans une question.

1 · Choose what or which

2 · Find what the relative refers to

3 · Build your own sentence

At home · Grammar 2 · about 15–20 minutes

Small words. Different meanings.

Que précise l’adverbe ?

Un adverbe peut préciser un verbe, un adjectif, un autre adverbe ou l’ensemble d’une proposition. Il faut identifier sa portée : l’élément sur lequel il agit.

ExemplePortée
Alice speaks politely.Politely précise le verbe speaks : comment parle-t-elle ?
The plan is very dangerous.Very intensifie l’adjectif dangerous.
Alice speaks very politely.Very intensifie l’adverbe politely.
Unfortunately, the plan fails.Unfortunately commente la proposition entière.

La place dépend du sens et du type d’adverbe

  • Fréquence : généralement avant le verbe lexical, mais après be. The Queen often threatens people. / Alice is usually polite.
  • Avec un auxiliaire : souvent entre l’auxiliaire et le verbe lexical. They have carefully painted the roses.
  • Manière : souvent après le verbe et son complément. Alice answers the Queen politely. On peut aussi rencontrer Alice politely answers the Queen.
  • Intensité : devant l’adjectif ou l’adverbe modifié. Very dangerous / very politely.

Évite de placer un adverbe de manière entre le verbe et son complément direct : préfère They paint the roses carefully à They paint carefully the roses.

Only : déplacer le mot change la restriction

Only Alice challenges the Queen. → Alice est la seule à la contester.
Alice challenges only the Queen. → Elle ne conteste que la Reine.

Beaucoup d’adverbes se forment avec adjectif + -ly, mais pas tous : often, very, soon sont aussi des adverbes. Certains mots en -ly, comme friendly, sont des adjectifs.

1 · Form and use adverbs

2 · Identify the scope or the natural position

3 · Make the meaning change

Use it in your news report

The Queen spoke impatiently. The gardeners are extremely worried. The professor presented his plan very confidently. Unfortunately, the plan would make life harder.
